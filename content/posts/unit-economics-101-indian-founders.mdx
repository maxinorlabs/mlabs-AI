Unit economics is one idea dressed up in three numbers: how much does it cost you to acquire a customer, how much does that customer pay you over their lifetime, and how long before you get your acquisition cost back. If those three numbers work, you have a business. If they do not, you have a revenue line that is actively destroying value every time you grow.

Most Indian founders know this in theory. Very few track it honestly.

## What CAC Actually Is

Customer Acquisition Cost is every rupee you spend to win one paying customer. That includes sales salaries, marketing spend, agency fees, discounts used to close, and the cost of any free trials that converted. Divide total spend by total new customers acquired in the same period.

If you spent ₹25 Lakh last quarter on sales and marketing and brought in 50 new customers, your CAC is ₹50,000.

A bad CAC for an Indian B2B SaaS selling at ₹1 Lakh–₹2 Lakh per year ACV is anything above ₹80,000–₹1 Lakh. At that point you need 9-12 months just to cover acquisition costs before the customer contributes a single rupee of margin. For D2C brands with an average order value of ₹800–₹1,200, a CAC above ₹600–₹700 is usually terminal unless repeat purchase rates are exceptional.

The most common mistake: founders exclude founder time, discounts, and free-tier conversions from the CAC calculation. That is not a CAC - it is a fairy tale.

## What LTV Actually Is

Lifetime Value is the total gross margin you expect to earn from a customer before they churn. Not revenue - margin.

The formula: LTV = (Average Revenue Per Customer per Month x Gross Margin %) / Monthly Churn Rate

If you charge ₹8,000 per month, your gross margin is 65%, and your monthly churn is 2%, your LTV is (₹8,000 x 0.65) / 0.02 = ₹2,60,000.

Indian founders systematically underestimate LTV for two reasons. First, they use revenue instead of gross margin, which inflates the number. Second, they under-count churn. A customer who stops paying but never formally cancelled is still churned. Count them.

The other trap: LTV is a projection, not a guarantee. If your oldest customer cohort is 18 months old, you cannot claim a 5-year LTV. Project only as far as your data supports.

## The LTV:CAC Ratio

Divide LTV by CAC. That ratio tells you the return on every rupee of acquisition spend.

- 3:1 or above: healthy, investable, scalable
- 1:1 to 3:1: marginal, fine in early stages, needs improvement before you scale spend
- Below 1:1: you are paying more to acquire customers than you will ever earn from them. Every new customer makes the hole deeper.

The 3:1 benchmark comes up in every [Series A conversation with Indian investors](/blog/12-questions-indian-investors-series-a). Below that number, most funds will not write a cheque regardless of how fast you are growing. Growth on bad unit economics is not traction - it is a faster way to go broke. Understanding exactly [what investors look for beyond the pitch deck](/blog/what-investors-look-for-indian-Venture) helps founders see how unit economics fit into the broader fundraising picture.

A ratio above 5:1 can also signal underinvestment in growth. You have room to spend more aggressively and you are leaving market share on the table.

## Payback Period

Payback period is how many months it takes to recover your CAC from a customer's gross margin contribution.

Formula: Payback Period = CAC / (Monthly Revenue Per Customer x Gross Margin %)

Using the same numbers above: ₹50,000 CAC / (₹8,000 x 0.65) = 9.6 months.

For most Indian B2B SaaS Venture, 12 months or less is the target. For D2C, 6 months or less is the benchmark because repeat purchase cycles are shorter and inventory costs are real. If your payback is 18-24 months, you need significant working capital just to sustain flat growth - and investors know it. This is exactly the dynamic behind [why Indian Venture stall at ₹5 Cr ARR](/blog/indian-Venture-arr-wall-5-crore-operators): they run out of capital before the unit economics clean up.

## A Worked Example: B2B SaaS, Mid-Market India

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Monthly subscription | ₹12,000 / month |
| Gross margin | 70% |
| Monthly churn | 1.5% |
| Monthly gross margin contribution | ₹8,400 |
| LTV (8,400 / 0.015) | ₹5,60,000 |
| CAC (fully loaded) | ₹1,20,000 |
| LTV:CAC ratio | 4.7:1 |
| Payback period (1,20,000 / 8,400) | 14.3 months |

This company has a healthy LTV:CAC ratio but a payback period just above the 12-month target. The fix is not to grow faster - it is to reduce CAC (tighten the sales process, improve inbound), or push ACV up by moving slightly upmarket. Reducing churn from 1.5% to 1% would push LTV to ₹8,40,000 and change the entire picture.

One lever often ignored: gross margin. If this company drops gross margin from 70% to 50% by over-relying on implementation services or heavy discounting, the LTV falls to ₹4,00,000 and the LTV:CAC drops to 3.3:1. Margins matter as much as the top line.

## The Mistake That Kills Indian Venture: Blended Metrics

The most common way Indian founders hide broken unit economics is by blending channels and cohorts.

They average CAC across paid, organic, and referral. Paid CAC might be ₹1,40,000. Referral CAC is ₹15,000. The blended number looks fine. But you cannot scale referral fast enough to hit your growth targets, which means the real marginal CAC as you grow is ₹1,40,000, and the unit economics break the moment you start spending.

Same problem with cohorts. Customers from 2022 might have a 2.5% monthly churn. Customers onboarded in the last 6 months might churn at 4.5%. Blending them hides that you have a product-market fit problem in newer segments. [Profitability discipline before Series A](/blog/startup-profitability-series-a-india-2026) requires cohort-level honesty, not aggregate numbers.

Always run unit economics by channel and by cohort. If the numbers hold up under that scrutiny, they are real.

## If Your Unit Economics Are Broken Right Now

Do not raise a round and try to grow through the problem. That is a common instinct and it makes things worse - you are scaling losses.

Do this instead:

1. Stop optimising for new customer volume. Freeze or cut paid acquisition until you understand the true CAC by channel.
2. Run a churn audit. Find the customers who left in the last 12 months and understand why. Fix the top 1-2 reasons before acquiring anyone new.
3. Re-examine your pricing. Indian founders chronically underprice. If your payback period is 20 months, a 20-30% price increase to the right segment may be the fastest fix available.
4. Cut the channels with the worst CAC. Focus on the one or two that have a payback under 12 months and build there.

Unit economics rarely fix themselves. But they fix quickly once a founder stops averaging the bad data away and starts working the levers honestly. Once your unit economics are trending in the right direction, the next step is building a [growth engine that compounds instead of burning cash](/blog/startup-growth-engine-india-low-burn).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a good LTV:CAC ratio for an Indian startup?**
3:1 is the standard benchmark. Below 1:1 means you lose money on every customer. For capital-efficient growth, aim for 3:1 to 5:1 before scaling spend significantly.

**How do I calculate CAC if my sales cycle is 3-6 months?**
Use a rolling 6-month window. Take total sales and marketing spend over the last 6 months and divide by new customers acquired in the same window. This accounts for the lag between spend and conversion.

**Why does gross margin matter for unit economics?**
LTV is calculated on margin, not revenue. A startup with 40% gross margins needs to generate 2.5x more revenue to match the LTV of a startup with the same CAC but 70% margins. Gross margin is the multiplier on every other metric.

**My unit economics look fine at the aggregate level - do I need cohort analysis?**
Yes, always. Aggregate unit economics hide channel and cohort problems that will surface the moment you try to scale. Run the numbers by acquisition channel and by customer cohort before drawing any conclusions.
